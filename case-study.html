    Usability Testing of a Multimedia Storytelling Tool

    Can readers use the interactive page types of a multimedia storytelling tool intuitively? A moderated usability study with 18 participants on desktop and mobile shows what came easily to them and where they ran into difficulties.

    Project
    Multimedia storytelling tool
    Master's project, RH Köln (then RFH)
    My Role
    Discussion guide (co-author), session moderation & note-taking
    Participant and questionnaire analysis
    Methods
    Moderated usability testing, think-aloud
    Semi-structured interview, post-test questionnaire
    Timeline
    2019
    Group project, usability lab
    01 · Context

    Multimedia storytelling with its own interaction logic

    The tested tool is used by a German media company to publish scroll-based multimedia stories. A single story combines up to ten page types: background video, collage, video, background image, text, hotspots, audio, before/after comparison, 360° image and a list of external links.

    As part of a cooperation between the university and the company, our group tested a sample story on street food. Two questions guided the study:

    Can the page types be used intuitively? And which obstacles do users run into while exploring them?
    02 · Method

    60 minutes per participant, from usage context to navigation tasks

    Two students ran each session: one person moderated, the other took notes. Participants thought aloud throughout. The discussion guide moved from open to specific:

    1
    Usage context · 10 min

    How, where, how often and on which device participants consume longer-form content.

    2
    Free exploration · 5 min

    First impression without any task, in the setting participants had described as their preferred one.

    3
    Page types in detail · 25 min

    One short task per page type, followed by probing questions on what worked and what didn't.

    4
    Global navigation · 10 min

    Orientation and navigation tasks: find chapter 2, hide the text, open the overview.

    5
    Post-test interview & questionnaire

    Where and why participants would have dropped off, favourite and least favourite page types, and five-point ratings on appearance, navigation, readability, labelling, learnability and speed.

    18participants
    (aged 19 to 58)

    Eleven women and seven men took part. All sessions took place in the usability lab at RH Köln. Participants could use a provided device or their own smartphone.

    8on desktop
    9on mobile
    (7 Android, 2 iOS)
    1on tablet

    For the analysis, insights were extracted per interview and ranked by the number of sessions in which they appeared.

    03 · What Worked

    Self-directed page types won

    Asked for their favourites, participants named hotspots (9), before/after (7) and the 360° image (6), all page types where the reader decides what to look at next. The plain text page was described as calm and tidy. Overall, participants understood the basic concept and enjoyed discovering hidden content.

    "I like that I can decide for myself what to look at first." M 24

    04 · Where It Broke Down

    Three of four navigation tasks broke down on mobile

    Participants enjoyed exploring the page types. The global navigation was a different story. Share of participants who could not complete each task:

    Hide the text 12 / 18
    Open the overview 10 / 18
    Tell where you are 9 / 18
    Go to chapter 2 8 / 18
    1
    Mobile
    Chapters without a visible start

    All eight participants who could not reach chapter 2 were on mobile. Once the colour coding of the navigation bar wasn't understood, nothing else on the page marked where a chapter begins. Most scrolled on hoping to stumble across it, then gave up.

    "Where is chapter 2?" W 32, mobile

    2
    Mobile
    A gesture nobody was told about

    On mobile, text is hidden by swiping left. Nothing on screen says so. Ten of the twelve failed attempts were on mobile, where the feature could only be found by trial and error. It also didn't work on every page type.

    3
    All devices
    A navigation bar read as a loading bar

    Half of the participants could not say where they were in the story. The green fill marking the current chapter was read as a progress or loading indicator.

    "Why is one half green? That could be mistaken for a progress marker." M 22, desktop

    4
    Page type
    Video behind text competes with reading

    16 of 18 participants found the background video distracting while reading. It was also the least liked page type. Autoplay, no pause and no direct mute came up repeatedly.

    "It's too much at once. Reading takes me three times as long." Participant

    The common thread: features that depend on an invisible gesture or an unexplained colour code weren't discovered. In Nielsen's terms, a gap in visibility of system status and a reliance on recall instead of recognition.
    05 · Expectations

    Readers wanted an overview before committing

    Seven participants missed an overview at the start, a kind of table of contents to judge whether the story was worth their time. Among those who described a mental model, a book came up most often, matching the wish for more depth of information.

    "What is this actually about? What do I get out of watching this?" W 54, desktop

    The commitment question confirmed it: only 4 of 18 would have watched the full story at home without condition, 7 only if interested in the topic, 7 not at all. Reasons for dropping off split between content, usability and length.

    At the client's request, we also tested the label "Multimedia-Reportage". 14 of 18 found it unsuitable: "multimedia" sounded dated, "Reportage" sounded like a TV news piece rather than something interactive.

    06 · Recommendations

    Make the hidden visible, starting with mobile

    #RecommendationAddresses
    1Add visible chapter markers to the mobile version and simplify chapter navigation8/18 could not reach chapter 2
    2Offer "hide text" directly on the page with a visible hint, and disable it where it doesn't work12/18 could not hide the text
    3Bring the overview page to mobile and add a table of contents at the start10/18 could not open it; 7/18 asked for an overview
    4Rework the colour coding of the navigation bar so it reads as position, not progress9/18 could not tell where they were
    5No autoplay with sound; give background video pause and mute controls16/18 found it distracting
    6Move calls to action from the bottom to the top of each page type; rename "Erste Seite" to "Home"Scroll hint too small and badly placed
    07 · My Part

    Participant groups and the post-test questionnaire

    Within the group, I analysed the participant structure and the post-test questionnaire, comparing ratings by device (desktop vs. mobile) and age group (younger vs. older).

    Recruiting through personal networks skewed the sample young, and finding older participants willing to travel to the lab for an hour was the main challenge. With eight to ten people per group, the comparisons served as directional signals that guided where to look in the qualitative data:

    • Older participants tended to rate learnability, navigation and visual appearance lower than younger ones.
    • Mobile participants rated the navigation bar and icons as harder to understand, which matches the task failures above.
    • Desktop participants rated navigation through the content as more frustrating.
    08 · Reflection

    What I would add today

    The study's strength was its structure: one guide, one task per page type, and the same navigation tasks for every participant, which made failures directly comparable across devices. Today I would add severity ratings to the findings so that the recommendations are prioritised by impact as well as frequency, and I would size any group comparison to the claim it has to support.

    The step I would add most of all is the one this portfolio is built around: prototyping the mobile chapter navigation in code and testing it again with the same tasks.
